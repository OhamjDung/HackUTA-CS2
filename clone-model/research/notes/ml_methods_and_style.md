# ML methods for game-sense-consistent imitation agents in Counter-Strike, and separating player quirks from shared pro technique

Reading key used in these notes:
- "Verified" = I read the primary source (paper full text or project page) in this session.
- "[Background, not re-fetched]" = from my prior knowledge of the paper; the URL is the canonical paper link, but I did not re-read it this session. The report writer should treat any such item as needing a check before it is stated as fact. I have kept these items qualitative and did not attach numbers to them.
- "[Speculation]" = my own reasoning about how a method could apply to the CS-NET v4 setup (the 138.7M-parameter transformer that predicts 4-second movement/view token sequences at 4 Hz, specialised per player with LoRA, which is low-rank adapter fine-tuning, plus a KL penalty to the base model, then expert iteration).

## 1. Durst et al. (2024), "Learning to Move Like Professional Counter-Strike Players" (MLMove)

### Takeaway
MLMove is a small (about 5.4M parameter) transformer that only predicts movement, uses no history, and was trained on 123 hours of pro CS:GO Retakes play on de_dust2. Human judges rated it 16% to 59% more human-like (by TrueSkill, a pairwise rating system) than rule-based and commercial bots, and it matched pro distributions of map occupancy and kill locations far better. Its strongest lessons for this project are: attention across all 10 players mattered, adding history made it worse, and it still turned corners poorly and was passive in some rounds.

### Cited Findings
- Venue and scope: Computer Graphics Forum 2024 (SCA 2024), arXiv 2408.13934. Mode is "Retakes" on de_dust2 only, weapons restricted, grenades disabled. Only movement is learned; aiming and firing are rule-based. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934); [Eurographics DL](https://diglib.eg.org/items/f55b53b1-3785-438b-91f8-1b21d8a4baa9/full)
- Inputs: one token per player (up to 10). Each token holds position, alive status, team, bomb site, seconds until the bomb explodes, plus derived features that approximate visibility and team strategy. Enemy positions are not a separate input; they arrive as the other players' tokens. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934)
- Architecture: three linear layers with LeakyReLU embed each token to 256 dimensions; four single-head self-attention layers of width 256, with masking for dead players; about 5.4M parameters (21 MB). — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934)
- Output: a 97-way discrete choice per player (16 directions × 3 speeds × jump/no-jump, plus standing still). It predicts actions for 0, 125 and 250 ms ahead and is queried every 16 game ticks (125 ms). — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934)
- No history input. The authors say this addresses the "inertia problem" (a model with history learns to just continue its previous action). — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934)
- Training data (CSKnow dataset): 123 hours at 16 Hz from over 17K rounds, 2,292 players, 513K shots, 29K eliminations, drawn from HLTV pro demos (1,156 hours of de_dust2, April 2021 to November 2022). Split 80/20 by round: 5.66M training points (98 h), 1.43M test points (25 h). — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934)
- Training: cross-entropy loss against movement labels that were inferred heuristically from position and velocity (not recorded keypresses); 20 epochs, batch 1024, Adam at learning rate 4e-5, position noise augmentation (variance 20 units); about 1.5 h on one RTX 4090. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934)
- Speed: about 8 ms per query on one Xeon core, about 0.5 ms amortized per game step for all players. The ablation section reports a 6.9 ms median, which does not match the 8 ms figure. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934); [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.13934)
- Human evaluation: 15 evaluators, including 5 "Global Elite" and 4 "Supreme Master First Class" (the top two CS:GO matchmaking ranks). They produced 120 rankings of four configurations (Human, MLMove, RuleMove = expert-scripted rules, GameBot = the commercial built-in bot). Rankings were turned into TrueSkill ratings. Kruskal-Wallis H = 333, p < 1e-5; all Dunn post-hoc tests p < 1e-5. MLMove was rated 16% to 59% more human-like than the baselines. Exact TrueSkill means are only in a figure. The paper gives conflicting round counts for the study (88 sampled rounds in one section, "eight rounds" in another). — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934)
- Self-play metrics, measured as Earth Mover's Distance (EMD, a distance between two distributions; lower means closer to pro play). MLMove / RuleMove / GameBot: map occupancy 8.2 / 14.7 / 15.2; kill locations 6.7 / 15.4 / 16.4; lifetimes 4.9 / 7.8 / 1.1; shots per kill 2.1 / 5.6 / 4.9. Note the lifetime row contradicts the paper's text, which says MLMove matched lifetimes best. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934)
- Teamwork metric, absolute % error versus pro counts of flanking and spreading configurations: offense 27% (MLMove) vs 55% (RuleMove) vs 58% (GameBot); defense 13% vs 42% vs 87%. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934)
- "Mistakes" metric (leaving high ground, abandoning a defensive position): MLMove close to human and well below the other bots; exact counts are only in a figure. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934)
- Ablations (EMD on occupancy / kill locations / lifetimes / shots-per-kill): default 8.2 / 6.7 / 4.9 / 2.1; no attention 10.3 / 8.2 / 4.6 / 2.2; with history 11.8 / 7.4 / 7.7 / 1.2. Removing attention between players hurt occupancy and kill-location match. Adding history hurt occupancy, kill locations and lifetimes. No ablation of team, visibility or time features was reported. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934)
- Stated limitations: one map, one mode; model under-turns corners; passive in some rounds; an exploratory study with humans playing against the bots was inconclusive because players were too absorbed in play to judge movement; labels are inferred. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934)
- Code and data: project page with the trained model, rule-based executor, CSKnow curation and Python evaluation at https://mlmove.github.io; author page at davidbdurst.com/mlmove/. No separate dataset download link appeared in the paper text. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934); [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.13934)

### Inferences
- [Speculation] MLMove's evaluation set (map occupancy, kill locations, lifetimes, shots per kill, flank/spread counts, "mistake" counts) can be reused almost directly on Mirage self-play as an objective human-likeness score, and its "mistake" counters (abandoning a held angle, leaving high ground) are a ready-made proxy for "poor angle-checking".
- [Speculation] The history ablation suggests a mechanism for "same tactic each round": a model that sees its own past tokens can lock into continuing them. CS-NET v4 predicts 4-second token sequences autoregressively, which has the same inertia risk. A check worth running: does diversity across rounds rise if the conditioning context drops the model's own recent tokens?
- MLMove's evidence is for movement only and for a short (250 ms) horizon; it does not show learned reaction to sound, since sound is not an input. — based on [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934)

### Gaps
- Per-baseline TrueSkill numbers and the exact mistake counts are only in figures I could not read as text.
- No feature ablation for team, enemy-visibility, or round-time features exists in the paper, so "which features mattered" is only answered for attention and history.

## 2. Pearce & Zhu (2021/2022), "Counter-Strike Deathmatch with Large-Scale Behavioural Cloning"

### Takeaway
A pixel-input behaviour-cloning agent (EfficientNetB0 plus a convolutional LSTM, 6.0M parameters) pre-trained on 95 hours of scraped online play and fine-tuned on 3.3 hours of clean expert play reached about casual-gamer level in deathmatch. Pre-training on the large noisy dataset was worth roughly 10× expert data. The paper did not run a Turing test; it judged human-likeness qualitatively and by map-coverage distance. Its listed failure modes (no audio so no reaction to footsteps, poor memory of enemies that go behind cover, ignores teammate cues) are close to the CS-NET v4 symptoms.

### Cited Findings
- Venue: IEEE Conference on Games (CoG) 2022, Best Paper Award; also NeurIPS 2021 Offline RL workshop. arXiv version has extra experiments not in the CoG version. — [GitHub README](https://github.com/TeaPearce/Counter-Strike_Behavioural_Cloning); [CoG paper PDF](https://ieee-cog.org/2022/assets/papers/paper_45.pdf); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.04258)
- Architecture: EfficientNetB0 (first six residual stages, ImageNet initialised) on a 280×150×3 crop, then a convolutional LSTM, then a linear output layer; 6.0M parameters; runs at 16 frames per second. — [CoG paper PDF](https://ieee-cog.org/2022/assets/papers/paper_45.pdf)
- Action space: WASD, fire, reload, jump as independent binary outputs; mouse x discretised into 19 uneven bins (finer near zero, from −300 to +300) and mouse y into 13 bins (−50 to +50). Treating mouse as continuous with mean-squared error "led to undesirable behaviour" (averaging between two paths), so they switched to classification. Movement keys and mouse are chosen by argmax because sampling produced "jerky, unnatural movement". — [CoG paper PDF](https://ieee-cog.org/2022/assets/papers/paper_45.pdf)
- Data: Stage 1, large online dataset scraped by spectating official servers: 680 GB, 95 hours, 5.5M frames, with actions inferred by a hand-tuned rule-based inverse-dynamics model from metadata. Stage 2, clean expert data recorded from a top-10% player: deathmatch 3.3 hours (190K frames), aim mode 0.8 hours (45K frames). — [CoG paper PDF](https://ieee-cog.org/2022/assets/papers/paper_45.pdf)
- Auxiliary output: the agent also predicts a value estimate with reward r = 1.0·kill − 0.5·death − 0.02·shoot, described as possibly providing extra supervision; it is not used otherwise. — [CoG paper PDF](https://ieee-cog.org/2022/assets/papers/paper_45.pdf)
- Results (kills per minute, KPM; kill/death ratio, K/D; mean ± standard error). Fine-tuned agent: easy deathmatch 5.06 KPM, 3.87 K/D; medium 3.72 KPM, 2.09 K/D; vs humans 1.43 KPM, 0.59 K/D. Online-only agent: 3.47 / 2.70, 2.23 / 1.04, 0.68 / 0.22. Built-in bots: 2.11 KPM (easy), 2.41 (medium). Human casual gamer: 4.20 / 4.20, 3.51 / 2.48, 1.64 / 0.64. Strong human: 14.00 / 11.67, 7.80 / 4.33, 4.27 / 2.34. Aim-train mode: fine-tuned agent 26.86 KPM vs casual human 26.21. Fine-tuning raised medium-mode KPM by about 45%. — [CoG paper PDF](https://ieee-cog.org/2022/assets/papers/paper_45.pdf)
- Pre-training value: fine-tuning from online-data pre-training beat ImageNet or random initialisation at every amount (1–3 h) of clean data; the authors extrapolate that about 20 hours of clean expert data would be needed from ImageNet init to match the pre-trained model fine-tuned on just 2 hours. — [CoG paper PDF](https://ieee-cog.org/2022/assets/papers/paper_45.pdf)
- Human-likeness evaluation: qualitative list plus map-coverage heatmaps on a 60×60 grid compared by Earth Mover's Distance. Online agent vs online dataset 0.447; fine-tuned agent vs clean dataset 0.349; built-in bot vs those datasets 0.624 / 0.643. The paper says Turing-style tests are "future work". — [CoG paper PDF](https://ieee-cog.org/2022/assets/papers/paper_45.pdf)
- Human-like traits observed: mouse pauses mid-turn as if at the edge of the mouse pad, runs along ledges, jumps to spot occluded areas; fine-tuned agent moves behind cover while reloading and strafes during fights. — [CoG paper PDF](https://ieee-cog.org/2022/assets/papers/paper_45.pdf)
- Non-human traits: poor memory ("if an enemy disappears behind cover it quickly forgets about it", still true after adding an LSTM layer); does not pick up "second-order clues" such as teammates firing in a direction; poor at vertical aim; poor navigation in rarely visited map areas; occasionally gets stuck. "The agent only receives the image as input, so has no audio clues that humans react to (shots being fired, or enemy footsteps)." — [CoG paper PDF](https://ieee-cog.org/2022/assets/papers/paper_45.pdf)
- Code, model, datasets: https://github.com/TeaPearce/Counter-Strike_Behavioural_Cloning. — [GitHub README](https://github.com/TeaPearce/Counter-Strike_Behavioural_Cloning)

### Inferences
- [Speculation] The "pre-train on broad data, fine-tune on narrow data" result maps directly onto CS-NET v4's base-then-LoRA design. Pearce's numbers suggest the base model, not the 47 per-player rounds, carries most of the competence; per-player adaptation should be kept small.
- [Speculation] Pearce's failure list ("no audio", "forgets enemies behind cover", "ignores teammate firing cues") are all missing-input or missing-memory problems rather than missing-reasoning problems. This supports adding sound and last-known-enemy features as inputs before trying training-method fixes.

### Gaps
- The arXiv version reportedly has extra experiments; I could not extract its text in this session, so I cannot confirm whether it adds a human Turing test.

## 3. Xenopoulos et al.: ESTA, awpy, win-probability valuation, economic decisions — and win probability as a judge

### Takeaway
Xenopoulos's group provides the tooling (awpy parser), a public pro dataset (ESTA: 1,558 pro games), and calibrated round win-probability models (XGBoost AUC 0.79; LightGBM log loss 0.415–0.442 per map). A win-probability model scores how much each event changes the round outcome, so it can act as a coarse judge of whether a bot's choices are tactically sound. It does not measure human-likeness or style.

### Cited Findings
- "Valuing Player Actions in Counter-Strike: Global Offensive" (2020): win-probability model from map, time since round start, team equipment value, players and HP remaining, bomb status/site, and each team's minimum graph distance to bomb sites. Trained on about 55M game states (Oct 2016–May 2019), tested on about 18M (Jun–Dec 2019). — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2011.01324); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.01324)
- Test results (log loss / Brier score / AUC): XGBoost 0.5353 / 0.1842 / 0.7913; CatBoost 0.5443 / 0.1875 / 0.7851; logistic regression 0.5539 / 0.1912 / 0.7743; map-average baseline 0.6917 / 0.2493 / 0.5303. XGBoost was also best calibrated. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2011.01324)
- Feature importance: team equipment value strongest, then HP remaining; players remaining lower; map alone weak but fifth in importance. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2011.01324)
- Win Probability Added (WPA): each damage event is valued by the change in predicted win probability before vs after; credited to attacker, debited to victim. WPA was more stable month to month (correlation 0.40) than ADR, KAST% or HLTV Rating 2.0, and less correlated with kill/death ratio (0.73) than Rating 2.0 (0.93). One highlighted kill swung win probability by 67%. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2011.01324)
- ESTA dataset (2022): 1,558 pro LAN games (Jan 2021–May 2022), 8.6M actions, 7.9M frames, 417K trajectories; frames parsed at 2 Hz (demos are 64/128 Hz); over 40 attributes per player including location, view direction, inventory; CC BY-SA 4.0. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.09861); [GitHub](https://github.com/pnxenopoulos/esta)
- ESTA win-prediction benchmarks: LightGBM, XGBoost, MLP on vector state; Deep Sets and Set Transformer on per-player sets. LightGBM had the lowest log loss on every map (Dust 2 0.415, Nuke 0.416, Mirage 0.430, Inferno 0.442). Set models used per-player location, velocity, view direction, HP, equipment; the authors argue they capture location-dependent situations (e.g. CT save after plant) that vector models misjudge. Metrics: log loss and expected calibration error; no AUC. — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.09861)
- Compatibility: ESTA data only works with awpy 1.x (`pip install awpy==1.3.1`); awpy 2.x is not compatible. awpy itself: https://github.com/pnxenopoulos/awpy. — [ESTA GitHub](https://github.com/pnxenopoulos/esta)
- "Optimal Team Economic Decisions in Counter-Strike" (IJCAI 2021 AISA workshop): game-level win-probability model at round start using scores, equipment, money and spending; finds a pattern of sub-optimal team spending; introduces Optimal Spending Error (OSE) to rank teams by distance from predicted optimal spending. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.12990v1)

### Inferences
- [Speculation] Use as a judge in expert iteration: when choosing which of the 8 sampled futures to keep, add a term for change in win probability (WPA of the sampled future, computed by a set-model trained on Mirage states). This rewards tactically sound futures, not only those closest to the player's path. Risk: a WPA term pulls behaviour toward "optimal" play and away from the player's quirks; keep it as a filter (reject futures with large negative WPA) rather than a maximiser.
- [Speculation] WPA-type models only see coarse state (HP, equipment, positions). They will not reward checking an angle or walking quietly unless those change modelled outcomes. They are a sanity filter for "dumb" futures, not a game-sense reward on their own.
- [Speculation] ESTA (2 Hz, 2021–22 CS:GO pro games, includes Mirage) is a possible extra pre-training source for the base model, since Pearce found large-data pre-training worth about 10× expert data. CS:GO vs CS2 movement differences (sub-tick, physics changes) would need checking.

### Gaps
- I did not find a paper that uses a CS win-probability model as an RL reward or as a selection judge for imitation agents.
- Exact OSE results and per-team rankings were not in the search results.

## 4. Other relevant methods: what each fixed, and how it could apply

### Takeaway
The closest recent CS-like work (Justesen et al., Dec 2024) adds directional audio sensors and ray-cast "vision" and reaches a near-coin-flip Turing test with 20 judges. For the "same tactic every round" problem, the best-documented fix is AlphaStar's conditioning on a sampled human strategy summary (z), with z dropped 10% of the time. Most other remedies (auxiliary prediction losses, discriminator rewards, best-of-N distillation, guidance) are well established in general ML, but I found no CS-specific measured results for them in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Justesen et al., "Human-like Bots for Tactical Shooters Using Compute-Efficient Sensors" (arXiv 2501.00078, Dec 2024), a CS-like tactical shooter in Unreal Engine 5:
  - Vision: 225 ray casts in a 15×15 grid, dense near the crosshair; each ray reports 10 object layers (enemies, teammates, grenades, smoke, fire, bomb, bomb site, geometry). Extra range finders for important objects within the 90° view. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00078)
  - Audio: 8 directional audio sensors for each of 5 sound types (footsteps/jumps, shots, bomb beeps, grenade explosions, bomb drops), using the UE5 sound engine with 100 m range. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00078)
  - Model: four input streams (convolution on rays, called "critical to success"), concatenated into an LSTM, multiple output heads; six sizes from about 0.6M to 36M parameters; model D about 14.9M. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00078)
  - Data: 28 players, about 48.6 hours of 2v2 play at LAN parties, about 2.8M timesteps at 16 Hz. Behaviour cloning only, no RL. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00078)
  - Turing test: 20 experienced gamers, 32 clips of 15 seconds (16 bot, 16 human). Human clips labelled human 60% of the time; bot clips labelled bot 54%, labelled human 30%, unsure 16%. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00078)
  - Statistics match: model D had the lowest Jensen-Shannon divergence (a distribution distance) to human data on most features and best heatmap match, but the worst movement-speed match; larger models fired fewer shots than humans; smallest model got stuck. Inference 9.59 ms (model D) vs 24.1 ms for a Pearce-style CS:GO model on CPU. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00078)
- AlphaStar strategy conditioning: the policy is conditioned on a statistic z summarising a human game's strategy (first 20 build-order items plus cumulative statistics), sampled from human data so the agent covers the different modes of human play. In supervised training z is zeroed 10% of the time. During RL a KL term keeps the policy near the supervised policy. — secondary summaries: [Yekun's notes](https://cyk1337.github.io/notes/2019/07/21/RL/DRL/Decipher-AlphaStar-on-StarCraft-II); [xlnwel blog](https://xlnwel.github.io/blog/reinforcement%20learning/AlphaStar); original: Vinyals et al. 2019, Nature, doi 10.1038/s41586-019-1724-z
- Follow-up measurement (TStarBot-X): a supervised agent beat the built-in Elite bot 90% of the time with either zero z or z sampled from 174 human z-statistics; without importance sampling it reached only 68%. — [arXiv 2011.13729](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.13729v1)
- StarCraft Commander: most performance came from 4,638 replays compared with the full 105,034. — as reported in [survey arXiv 2111.07631](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.07631); I did not read the primary paper.
- Fine-tuning on higher-skill replays (MMR above 6200) improved the policy by about 9 percentage points against the built-in elite bot. — as reported in [survey arXiv 2111.07631](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.07631); not read in primary form.
- Play-style-specific cloning: Ingram et al. cluster trajectories by behaviour and train a cloned policy per cluster, to get diverse play-style agents. — [RAIL lab PDF](https://www.raillab.org/publication/ingram-2023-creating/ingram-2023-creating.pdf) (search snippet only, not read in full)
- VizDoom behaviour cloning of individual players: models trained on players with different styles became aggressive, passive, or more human-like than traditional AI. — [arXiv 2401.03993](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03993) (abstract-level only)
- [Background, not re-fetched] Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL), which trains a discriminator to tell expert from agent behaviour and uses its output as reward: [arXiv 1606.03476](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.03476). The NTT paper notes its SYM-FF classifier is "equivalent to typical discriminator architectures in adversarial imitation learning". — [NTT paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.09637)
- [Background, not re-fetched] DAgger (dataset aggregation: roll out the learner, have the expert relabel the states it visits, retrain) fixes compounding error from distribution shift; needs a queryable expert. — [Ross et al. 2011 PDF](http://www.ri.cmu.edu/pub_files/2011/4/Ross-AISTATS11-NoRegret.pdf)
- [Background, not re-fetched] Expert iteration (Anthony et al. 2017): a search procedure generates improved actions and a network is trained to imitate them. — [arXiv 1705.08439](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439). RAFT (reward-ranked fine-tuning: sample N outputs, keep the best by a reward model, fine-tune on them) is the same idea for generative models. — [arXiv 2304.06767](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.06767)
- [Background, not re-fetched] KL-regularised search toward a human imitation policy ("piKL") was proposed to get play that is both strong and predictive of human moves. — [arXiv 2112.07544](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07544)
- [Background, not re-fetched] Multimodal action models for behaviour cloning that avoid averaging between modes: Behavior Transformers (discretise actions with k-means plus a residual offset) — [arXiv 2206.11251](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.11251); Diffusion Policy — [arXiv 2303.04137](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04137). Classifier-free guidance (train with and without a condition, then extrapolate between the two at sampling time) — [arXiv 2207.12598](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.12598).

### Inferences
- [Speculation] "No reaction to sound or sighting": Pearce lists missing audio as a cause; Justesen fixes it with directional per-sound-type inputs. CS-NET v4's inputs (state, depth rays, positions) as described do not include sound events. Adding per-player "heard footstep / shot / utility from direction d within the last t seconds" tokens, derived from demo events (awpy parses weapon fires and other events; footstep audibility would need to be simulated from enemy speed and distance), is the most direct fix. Visibility ("enemy in view now", "last seen position and time") is the equivalent fix for sightings and for Pearce's "forgets enemies behind cover" failure.
- [Speculation] Auxiliary losses: have the model also predict (a) where enemies are now, given only what the player could know, and (b) whether a sound event will occur in the next second. These force internal representations of hidden enemy state without training explicit reasoning. Pearce's value-head auxiliary is a precedent for adding extra prediction heads in a CS cloning agent, but no ablation of its effect was reported. I found no CS paper that measures the gain from enemy-position auxiliary losses.
- [Speculation] "Same tactic each round": the current expert-iteration selection keeps the one future out of 8 closest to the real path. That is best-of-N toward a single target per state, which narrows the output distribution each iteration (mode collapse). AlphaStar's answer is to condition on a sampled strategy summary z. A CS analogue: z = a summary of the player's round (e.g. which site/lane taken in the first 20 s, first utility thrown, buy type), sampled from that player's 47 rounds at play time, zeroed 10% of the time in training so the model also works unconditioned. Classifier-free guidance on z could then strengthen the chosen tactic.
- [Speculation] "Loud running": this is a measurable statistic (fraction of time at run speed when enemies are within audible range). AlphaStar-style z can include it, and a statistics-matching check (as in MLMove/Justesen) can measure it. A filter in expert iteration that rejects futures exceeding the player's own walk/run ratio in similar states would target it directly.
- [Speculation] "View turning drifted away from the player's": if the expert-iteration selection distance is computed on path (position) only, then view tokens are never selected for, and drift is expected. Include view-angle error in the selection distance, and add or raise the KL-to-base term specifically on view tokens.
- [Speculation] DAgger needs an expert to relabel bot-visited states; a pro cannot be queried. The closest proxy is the base model or a separately trained "population" model as the relabeller, which is in effect what the KL-to-base term already does.
- [Speculation] GAIL-style discriminator reward: with only 47 rounds per player, a per-player discriminator will overfit quickly; a population-level discriminator (human pro vs bot, trained on all pro data) is more realistic and doubles as an evaluation metric (see section 5).

### Gaps
- I found no CS:GO/CS2 paper from 2025–2026 that learns tactics (not just movement) from pro data with a measured human-likeness result; the search returned only MLMove and Justesen et al.
- No measured CS-specific results for auxiliary losses, GAIL, CFG, best-of-N distillation, or entropy bonuses were found in this session. Original AlphaStar ablation numbers for z were not retrieved.

## 5. Evaluating human-likeness and "game sense" objectively

### Takeaway
Human Turing-style judgements are noisy: in BotPrize 2012, humans were rated only about 41% human while the best bots scored about 52%. A learned classifier (the "automated Navigation Turing Test") can tell humans from agents with up to 85% accuracy, but it does not reliably rank which agent is more human-like. Use three layers together: behaviour-statistic distances against pro data, a population human-vs-bot classifier, and a small blind human study with side-by-side clips.

### Cited Findings
- 2K BotPrize 2012 (Unreal Tournament 2004, judges tag opponents with a "judging gun"): MirrorBot 52.2% and UT^2 51.9% humanness, while the four human players averaged 41.4%. — [Singularity Hub](https://singularityhub.com/2012/10/08/turing-test-prize-has-two-winners/); [UT Austin news](https://news.utexas.edu/2012/09/26/artificially-intelligent-game-bots-pass-the-turing-test-on-turings-centenary/)
- Critique: humans should be near 100% humanness in a valid test; the 40% human score shows the judges failed to recognise human traits, and the contest measured NPC humanness only in a narrow setting. — [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/how-did-this-game-bot-score-higher-than-humans-on-a-tur-5947796)
- Navigation Turing Test (Devlin et al., ICML 2021), in a Ninja Theory AAA game (Bleeding Edge, per the paper's acknowledgement): 60 human judges, 10 side-by-side video pairs each, "which is more likely to be human?" plus a 5-point certainty scale. Judges picked the real human over an RL agent with mean accuracy 0.84 (Study 1) and 0.77 (Study 2). When comparing two agents, judges preferred the hybrid (depth-image) agent 0.78 (Study 1) vs 0.62 (Study 2) of the time; the difference between studies was significant, suggesting context or anchoring effects. — [NTT PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.09637)
- Automated NTT classifiers trained to separate human vs agent trajectories: identity accuracy from 0.583 (top-down trajectory CNN) to 0.850 (both symbolic-input models: feed-forward and GRU on positions). Agreement with human judgement on human-vs-agent pairs was high; on agent-vs-agent pairs it was low (about 0.2–0.5 accuracy) and Spearman rank correlations were negative (about −0.1 to −0.25). The authors conclude that predicting finer-grained human-likeness rankings "remains unsolved". Data and code: https://github.com/microsoft/NTT. — [NTT PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.09637); [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.09637)
- Simple symbolic (position-only) classifiers were more robust to overfitting than video-based ones. — [NTT PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.09637)
- MLMove: ranked comparisons converted to TrueSkill by 15 high-rank evaluators, plus EMD-based self-play statistics that the authors report correlate with evaluator judgements. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934); [Liner review summary](https://liner.com/review/learning-to-move-like-professional-counterstrike-players)
- Justesen et al.: 20 judges, 15-second clips, yes/no/unsure labels; 30% of bot clips judged human vs 60% of human clips judged human. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00078)
- Pearce & Zhu: map-coverage EMD on a 60×60 grid. — [CoG paper PDF](https://ieee-cog.org/2022/assets/papers/paper_45.pdf)
- MLMove found that judges playing against the bots were too absorbed to judge movement; watching replays worked better. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.13934)

### Inferences
- [Speculation] For "game sense" specifically, define situation-triggered statistics measured against the same player's real rounds: reaction latency (time from first audible enemy sound or first sighting to a change in movement or view); fraction of time walking vs running when enemies are within audible range; pre-aim accuracy (view angle error toward the most likely enemy position before contact, which is the "angle-checking" metric); round-to-round tactic entropy (how many distinct opening routes per 47 rounds vs the player's own count). These are behaviour-statistics matches of the MLMove/Justesen kind, aimed at the reported failures.
- [Speculation] Use a population human-vs-bot classifier as a cheap regression test between training runs (NTT shows these work for human-vs-agent), but do not use it to rank two bot versions against each other (NTT shows that fails).
- [Speculation] For the human study, use side-by-side replay clips judged by experienced CS players with a certainty scale (NTT and MLMove both did this), and include human-vs-human control pairs to measure the judges' baseline (the BotPrize lesson).

## 6. Player style vs shared technique

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence comes from chess (personalised Maia): fine-tuning a population model per player raised move-prediction accuracy by 4–5 points and identified the player among 400 with 98% accuracy from 100 games. But personalisation lost to the base model at 1,000 games per player (0.497 vs 0.527 accuracy), helped from 5,000 games upward, and was weakest for top-level players. With about 47 rounds per player, CS-NET v4 is far below the data level where full per-player fine-tuning helped in chess, so per-player changes should be small residuals on the population model, focused on the measurable places where the player differs.

### Cited Findings
- McIlroy-Young et al., "Learning Models of Individual Behavior in Chess" (KDD 2022): start from Maia (a population model of human chess moves) and fine-tune per player. Starting from Maia 1900 worked as well as rating-matched bases; freezing layers "only hurts performance"; random initialisation was much worse; most improvement came within the first 12,000 steps. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.10086); [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.10086)
- Accuracy gain: personalised models were 4–5 percentage points more accurate on average than the best-matching population model per player; perplexity on the player's moves 1.95 vs 2.15. They were better across all move qualities, from optimal moves to blunders. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.10086)
- Data needed (move-matching accuracy, nearest population Maia vs personalised): 1,000 games 0.527 vs 0.497 (personalisation worse); 5,000 games 0.532 vs 0.550; 10,000 0.528 vs 0.556; 20,000 0.524 vs 0.564; 30,000 0.520 vs 0.567; 40,000 0.528 vs 0.580. The authors attribute the 1,000-game failure to overfitting and suggest pooling data across similar players as future work. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.10086)
- Top players: "preliminary attempts to create personalized models for Grandmasters did not yield the same accuracy gains", conjectured because the strongest players play near-optimal moves. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.10086)
- Stylometry (identifying the player from moves alone, using whichever personalised model predicts best): top-1 accuracy among 400 players of 98% with 100 games (all moves), 86% with 10 games, 94% with 30 games; at move 30+ only, 55% with 100 games and 11% with 10 games (chance 0.25%). A naive Bayes baseline on per-move error vectors reached only 1.4%. Restricting to blunders raised accuracy slightly (0.983 to 0.989): mistakes are the most discriminative moves. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.10086)
- Bound: with a 4–5% accuracy edge, about 2,400·(ln 400 + ln 100) < 20,000 moves are enough for 90% identification confidence among 400 players. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.10086)
- Separating individual from population: plotting probability under the personalised model vs under the population model per move shows where the player differs; the log-odds ratio of the two identifies "idiosyncratic" moves. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.10086)
- Pre-training plus small fine-tune in CS: online pre-training plus 2 h of expert data was worth about 20 h of expert data from ImageNet init; fine-tuning on one strong player's data improved KPM by about 45% ("converging on a single high-skill policy"). — [CoG paper PDF](https://ieee-cog.org/2022/assets/papers/paper_45.pdf)
- AlphaStar sampled z from many human games so one policy can express many human strategies; conditioning on a strategy statistic is a way to separate "what strategy" from "how to execute". — [Yekun's notes](https://cyk1337.github.io/notes/2019/07/21/RL/DRL/Decipher-AlphaStar-on-StarCraft-II)
- A 2025 playstyle survey argues expressive playstyles require moving beyond score maximisation. — [arXiv 2508.19152](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.19152) (abstract-level only)
- Per-cluster cloning to get distinct play styles: [Ingram et al. 2023](https://www.raillab.org/publication/ingram-2023-creating/ingram-2023-creating.pdf) (snippet only).

### Inferences
- [Speculation] Data scale warning: 47 CS rounds is a tiny dataset compared to the 5,000+ chess games where personalisation started to beat the population model. Expect LoRA plus expert iteration on 47 rounds to overfit unless tightly regularised. The chess result that personalisation helped least for top players is also relevant, since donk and broky are top pros whose technique overlaps heavily with the population.
- [Speculation] Residual (delta) framing that fits the existing design: keep the population model as the default (already done via KL-to-base), and make the per-player LoRA carry only the difference. Measure the difference directly using the Maia log-odds idea: for each state in the player's rounds, compute log p_player(action) − log p_base(action). States with large positive values are the player's quirks; states near zero are shared technique. Weight the per-player loss toward high-log-odds states (prioritise quirks) and rely on KL-to-base elsewhere (keep shared technique).
- [Speculation] z-scores vs population: compute per-player behaviour statistics (run/walk ratio near enemies, opening route frequencies, peek timing, pre-aim angles, utility usage) and compare with the distribution across all pros. Statistics more than about 1.5–2 standard deviations from the pro mean are candidate quirks to condition on or to match in evaluation; statistics near the mean should come from the base model.
- [Speculation] Stylometry as a style metric: train a classifier (or use the per-player models, as in Maia) to identify which pro produced a round. A good clone should be identified as its player; a clone identified as "generic pro" has lost the quirks. This complements the population human-vs-bot classifier from section 5, which checks shared human-likeness.
- [Speculation] Pool across similar players (the Maia authors' suggestion for low-data players): train a style embedding per player on all pros' data jointly, so the 47 rounds of donk shift a learned embedding instead of tuning a full adapter. This is the "style embedding" route and uses far fewer free parameters per player.
